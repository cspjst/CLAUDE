# PRESS — how PRESS writes

*Owned by PRESS. A framework for blog posts and similar public writing,
drawn from four sources (below) and from synergy's own rules. Written
2026-10-31 at Jeremy's request (17:35): "perhaps you could take a look
and find others to synthesize your own framework for blog entries and
similar". Version 0.1: a proposal; Jeremy decides and rewrites. The
code of conduct wins over anything here.*

**Sign-off:** not yet. PRESS's frameworks are signed off by its human
owner, Jeremy (2026-10-31 18:06). Until then, scores against this
framework are provisional.

## The short version
1. **Know the reader, and the one thing they should leave with.**
2. **Open with the problem, in a situation they'd recognise.**
3. **Say early what the piece is about; then tell the story.**
4. **Evidence from the record, quoted exactly; thin parts labelled.**
5. **Always the "so what" and the "but what": the use, and the limits.**
6. **Plain words. Define a term the first time it appears, or drop it.**
7. **Clarity before catchiness. A catchy headline gets a plain subtitle.**
8. **Check everything before Jeremy sees it.**

## 1. Before writing: the Message Box
Fill this in first, in a sentence each, for one named reader. If a
box won't fill, the piece isn't ready. (COMPASS's Message Box.)
- **Issue:** the big topic, as a short phrase.
- **Problem:** the part of it this piece is about.
- **So what:** why *this* reader should care. It changes with the
  reader.
- **Solution:** what synergy did about it.
- **Benefit:** what the reader gets if they try it: concrete, who,
  where, when.

Two more questions from the In-Mind guide:
- **Why write it at all?** What should change for the reader?
- **The dinner-table test:** would the topic start a conversation with
  someone outside the field?

Scope: one idea per piece, broad enough to matter and narrow enough to
finish. A topic too big for one post becomes a series.

## 2. Shape: the hourglass
- **The lead (a few sentences):** the problem, and why to read on.
  "The first paragraph you write is by far the most important" (Simple
  Talk). Don't build suspense at the cost of clarity.
- **Then the story,** in order of events, because synergy's material is
  a history, and a story told in order is easy to follow. Carry it to
  the end: readers want to know how it came out.
- **Then the end:** what it adds up to (section 5).

This is the inverted pyramid at the top and narrative below it. A pure
inverted pyramid suits news, not features.

## 3. Evidence: synergy's own rules
These are what PRESS adds to the sources. They come from the code of
conduct and the operating model.
- **The record is the source.** The historiography first, then the
  working agreement, the dialogue and git. Nothing from memory.
- **Quotes exact,** with date and time, checked word for word against
  the oracle. Jeremy's wording is never tidied.
- **Thin is labelled thin.** If the record is silent, say so; don't
  fill the gap.
- **No claims about Claude beyond what synergy has observed.** "In
  synergy, a new session starts knowing nothing", not "AIs forget".
- **Say how strong the evidence is.** One human, one AI, a few days or
  weeks: say so. Cautious verbs where they're due ("so far", "in our
  case").
- **Commands and steps are run before they're written down.**
- **Names:** only with Jeremy's clearance. Otherwise "a friend".

## 4. Language
- Plain words; short sentences; active voice.
- **Write for the least-informed reader who'd still plausibly read
  it** (Simple Talk), and decide what they already know.
- **Define a term at first use, or cut it.** Jargon that survives
  needs a reason.
- **Metaphors sparingly, and with their limits stated** (In-Mind).
  One recurring image works better than several.
- **Examples real and labelled.** Synergy has real ones; prefer them.
- **Dry humour welcome; padding isn't** (the code of conduct). No
  emoji, slang or showing off.
- **For readers anywhere:** UK times stated as such; no assumptions
  about holidays, schooling or meal customs; no pop-culture references
  a reader might not share.
- **PRESS's own voice:** Claude, writing as PRESS, owning its work
  (Jeremy, 17:23). Never Jeremy's voice.

## 5. Endings: the "so what" and the "but what"
From the In-Mind guide, step 8:
- **So what:** the takeaways, in a short closing section. Who they
  apply to, and under what conditions. Practical advice only where the
  record supports it.
- **But what:** what synergy can't show, alternative explanations, and
  what hasn't worked. For synergy this is a standing rule, not an
  option: every piece is honest about failure.
- **Land gracefully:** recap without a checklist, and leave one
  pointer for further reading (Simple Talk).

## 6. Headlines
The sources pull two ways here. In-Mind says "Clarity before
catchiness": headlines should be informative and searchable, without
rhetorical questions, quotations or pop-culture references. A blog
headline also has to make someone stop scrolling. PRESS's rule:
- **A catchy headline is allowed only with a plain subtitle** that says
  exactly what the piece is about, in words people would search for.
- **A metaphor in the headline gets its limit stated** near the top.
- **No pop-culture references, no rhetorical questions.**

So "Your AI is a goldfish" passes: the subtitle says what the piece is
about, and the bracket underneath states the metaphor's limit. "Fifty
first dates with an AI" wouldn't pass: it depends on a film the reader
may not know.

## 7. Pictures
- **To show a process or a structure,** never as decoration.
- **The text in the picture says the same as the text beside it.**
- **Rendered and looked at before committing;** readable in light and
  dark mode.

## 8. The check before Jeremy sees it
- [ ] The Message Box fills, for a named reader.
- [ ] The lead states the problem and why to read on.
- [ ] Every quote matches the oracle word for word.
- [ ] Every date and time comes from the record; times are from the
      clock, never a guess.
- [ ] Every term is defined at first use, or cut.
- [ ] Each metaphor has its limit stated.
- [ ] The strength of the evidence is stated.
- [ ] There's a "so what" and a "but what", including what hasn't
      worked.
- [ ] No names without clearance, and nothing private.
- [ ] The headline, subtitle and opening all carry the same message.
- [ ] Commands and links tested.
- [ ] A reader from outside the field could follow it. When possible,
      ask one.
